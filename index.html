Oro Negro:
Mapping the geography of Venezuela’s oil

Venezuela has long been a subject of media attention. However, starting in early 2026, the focus shifted: it is now primarily centered on our vast endowment of resources. Indeed, we possess the world’s largest proven oil reserves, exceeding 303 billion barrels. However, not everyone is familiar with the story of our oil–and the pride Venezuelans take in our heritage–nor where the oil is located, how it is refined, or whether our electrical grid is capable of supporting such operations.

I decided to conduct an exploratory analysis to gather all relevant information in one place. The project’s objective is to provide a resource that allows for the mapping of these assets, the monitoring of system operations, and to raise awareness regarding existing limitations, as well as how these might be addressed.

A brief history

The two most famous and consequential oil blowouts (reventones) in Venezuela’s history occurred in Zulia State in the early 1900s, shaping the country’s economic and social trajectory.

The data and design

I used Python (pandas, GeoPandas) through Colab to clean and merge datasets, as well as Altair, Vega-Lite and Visual Studio Code to create all visualizations. The workbooks and .json files can be accessed in the repository.

The design of the website is inspired by the Venezuelan flag and the kinetic and optical art of Carlos Cruz-Diez, both representing an extension of our identity.

How all the data was managed

The data used includes geospatial datasets and oil industry data obtained from Geoportal Provita, GEM, OPEC, EIA, IEA, Global Solar Atlas, Global Wind Atlas and NASA. The data is supplemented and cross-checked by public sources and satellite data.

The resource

Notes for replication

The data is extracted from OPEC’s Annual Statistical Bulletin 2026. The share is calculated using Venezuela’s production divided by the world total for the same year, times 100, rounded to two decimals. Each year carries a historical note and are classified into four periods, coming from my own research and general background knowledge of Venezuelan history.

The geography of oil

The interactive map below tracks Venezuela’s oil blocks and fields by region. Field size can be viewed by oil in place (OPI) (the total estimated volume of crude physically trapped underground) and remaining recoverable (the fraction that can actually be brought to the surface using current technology). The data reveals that Zulia state holds the largest OIP volume at 266,563 million barrels (bbl) across 35 blocks and 25 fields, with 10,587 million bbl recoverable.

Blocks: Ministry of Energy and Petroleum via ECOSIG/Provita (records updated 2009 to 2018). Fields and reserves: Global Energy Monitor (GEM), Global Oil and Gas Extraction Tracker, March 2026 (reserve figures mostly from 2021). Some field locations added by the author.

Notes for replication

The state boundaries and oil blocks come from Provita. All names were translated from Spanish into English, preserving all special characters. The following blocks were placed manually to correct the location due to overlapping state boundaries: Mene Grande and Catatumbo in Zulia, La Ceiba in Trujillo, and Moruy blocks I and IV as offshore.

34 fields had no coordinates (latitude and longitude), so these were added manually, based on satellite data. Each field was matched to a block: first by lying inside it, then by the operator’s name, then by the nearest block.

Blocks and fields were grouped into regions to make the map easier to read for an international audience.

Our reserves

The chart illustrates the volume of proven oil reserves (in billions of bbl) for the top ten nations from 1960 to 2025. The data highlights Venezuela’s ascent from being a minor participant to the world leader, following the exploration of the Orinoco Oil Belt in 2010. Although OPEC’s reliance on self-reported data from member nations is frequently contested, a 2009 U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) fact sheet validates the claim, and estimates that the Orinoco Belt alone contains between 380 and 652 billion bbl of ultimately recoverable oil.

The system

The infrastructure network

The interactive map below tracks the refineries, upgraders and pipelines by region. The Paraguaná Refinery Complex is the largest in the Western Hemisphere and the second-largest in the world, with a nameplate capacity of 955,000 barrels per day (b/d). It encompasses the Amuay and Cardón refineries in Falcón state and the Bajo Grande refinery in Zulia state.

Refineries and upgraders: Wikipedia (Paraguaná refinery complex), OverWatts, Reuters (2023; February 2026, El Palito and refineries in July 2026, Cardón in October 2026), La Patilla, Ursa Space; capacities are nameplate. Notes for replication

Each item was placed in a region based on its coordinates, following the same rules as visualization #2. The GEM trackers were filtered to all those running in Venezuela (i.e., excluding two gas pipelines running across borders, Trans-Caribbean and Dragon, and Venezuela-Colombia which was cancelled). All routes were simplified to about 200 meters.

The location of the refineries and upgraders were double-checked with public sources. All installed capacities are estimated, nameplate, not what they process. To improve the visualization, Amuay and Cardón are shown when zooming to Falcón and Paraguaná, given both are part of the Paraguaná Refinery Complex. The four upgraders at José in Anzoátegui share one location, so they are one dot on the map.

The constraint

The refining gap

The chart illustrates the historical gap between Venezuela’s oil refining capacity and its actual throughput (the volume of crude oil processed). The data reveals that this deficit has widened significantly over decades, coming closest to closing only in 1991. This disparity is attributed to constrains fueled by five key issues: underinvestment, neglected maintenance, widespread equipment damage, frequent power disruptions, and critical material shortages.

Sources: Energy Institute (2026), Statistical Review of World Energy 2026, Energy Institute, London (refining capacity and refinery throughput, Venezuela, 1980 to 2025).

Notes for replication

The data comes from the Energy Institute’s Statistical Review of World Energy 2026. Capacity starts in 1965 and throughput in 1980, so the chart’s range is from 1980 to 2025. Capacity not in use (gap) was calculated as capacity minus throughput, and the share in use is throughput divided by capacity.

The electricity constraint

The chart below illustrates the fragility of Venezuela’s energy sector, exposing a severe historical gap between capacity and actual electricity generation. As most of the power comes from the Guri hydropower plant, the entire grid remains highly vulnerable, leading to frequent nationwide blackouts. Despite its vast natural resources, Zulia state is one of the most affected. Reports from 2026 indicate that daily blackouts of up to eight hours and constant power fluctuations, which affects 99% of its population.

This mismatch is critical, as industrial operations recover, and oil production is expected to expand. The main issue is that operations may demand more than what the current grid can sustain, triggering more shortages.

Installed capacity and power generated, 1980–2024: U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), International Energy Statistics, Venezuela, electricity (net installed capacity and net generation: total, fossil fuels and hydroelectricity). Cross-check of generation: Energy Institute, Statistical Review of World Energy 2026. Late 2026: Noris Hernández, “Crisis del Sistema Eléctrico Nacional mantiene al país parado sobre una bomba de tiempo”, El Regional del Zulia, 15 November 2026. The late-2026 figures are a press estimate, not official data. On the reliability of Venezuela’s electricity statistics: Chatham House (2021).

Notes for replication

The left panel draws data for installed capacity and power generated coming from the EIA. Energy generation data was compared year by year from 1985 to 2024 with the Energy Institute’s data. The median difference in total generation is 2.5%.

Capacity is measured in million kW (= GW), and generation in billion kWh (= TWh). To compare them, generation was converted to average power: TWh in a year divided by 8.76 (the thousands of hours in a year) gives GW. “Capacity not producing power” is installed capacity minus average power generated.

The right panel shows operating capacity at a specific time in 2026 coming from a newspaper article from El Regional del Zulia. The article indicates a total installed capacity is 33,238 MW (18,006 MW thermoelectric, 15,137 MW hydroelectric, and 95 MW solar/wind). However, only 11,000–14,000 MW is actually delivered—just 33%–42% of total capacity—falling short of the 15,700–16,300 MW demand by over 3,000 MW. On the chart, solid bars represent minimum estimates while thin lines extend to the maximums, and solar/wind figures (95 MW installed; 0.40 MW operating) appear as flat lines because they are too small for a 36,000 MW scale.

Can satellite data reveal how power constraints reshaped Venezuela’s oil sector?

To visualize how the electricity constraint impacted oil production, I tested whether nighttime light from space tracks refinery activity. Normally, we would expect that brighter lights would mean higher industrial activity, but by looking at the output, in Venezuela I found the opposite: between 2012 and 2025, light tripled while crude processing fell 70% (a -0.90 correlation). Actually, in the years where more crude was processed, there was less light.

To find out why, I split the light around each refinery into three zones: the plant, its gas flares, and the surrounding area, to build an interactive map to explore the zones by year and consider the activity.

Night-time light: NASA Black Marble, product VNP46A4 (annual composite), from the VIIRS instrument on the Suomi NPP satellite, obtained from NASA’s LAADS DAAC. Reference: Román et al. (2018). Refinery throughput: Energy Institute, Statistical Review of World Energy 2026 (includes data from ICIS and S&P Global Energy). Locations of the refineries and upgraders (approximate): Wikipedia, the ends of Global Energy Monitor pipelines, and Mapcarta. Blackouts named under the year: March 2019 and 30 August 2024 (Associated Press, via PBS NewsHour). Country outline: boundary file supplied with the Global Solar Atlas download (World Bank Group). State boundaries and the Guayana Esequiba: Instituto Geográfico de Venezuela Simón Bolívar (IGVSB), via Provita’s geoportal. The light was grouped into three zones around each plant and shown as a percentage of each place’s own usual level.

Notes for replication

The base map comes from Provita. The satellite data comes from NASA Black Marble, which processes night-time images of the VIIRS instrument on the Suomi NPP satellite. The dataset used is VNP46A4, which provides the image correction for clouds, moonlight and the atmosphere and combines the nights of a year into one annual picture. The data was downloaded with NASA Earthdata access and the open-source package blackmarblepy, for the layer of the annual product that the package returns by default.

The map shows the main refineries as mapped by GEM and the throughput from the Energy Institute, following visualization #4 and #5 respectively. To differentiate between plant light, gas flaring (single brightest pixel) and the surrounding area (ring from 5 to 10 km, averaged per pixel, not counting the plant), the data was divided into three zones. Around each place, the plant is a disc of 1.5 km. A pixel counts if its center falls inside the shape. Gaps, clouds and fill values are left out.

Each zone is shown as a percentage of that area’s usual bright level: 100% is the 95th percentile of its own yearly values, and the number never goes above 100. A place is compared with itself, not with other places. Lit means 60% or more, dim 30 to 60%, and dark under 30%; flaring is “strong”, “some” or “little” on the same thresholds.

The satellite picture is the light of the selected view for the selected year, on a logarithmic scale so that towns and flares both show. The whole-country view has about 1.3 km per pixel and the closer view of the refinery coast about 0.7 km. All years can be compared, as all have the same scale.

Bajo Grande is inside Maracaibo, and Puerto La Cruz and San Roque are next to towns. For this reason, consider that their light includes the towns’ light. Additionally, a blackout of days or weeks barely changes it when using annual picture averages; to improve this map, month-by-month data would be needed.

For the periods, the average is the mean of the yearly percentages in the period; the swing is their standard deviation, in percentage points, so it measures how much one year differs from another.

The proposal

Venezuela possesses vast, untapped renewable energy potential. Spanning the states of Zulia, Falcón, Lara, Guárico, Anzoátegui, Sucre, and Nueva Esparta, offer a localized mix of wind and solar resource bands to diversify the national grid and support oil production. While this mapping does not determine final feasibility or where plants can be built, it serves as a visual catalyst to spark discussions on infrastructure modernization.

Solar Power

The map covers the long-term average of the electricity a photovoltaic plant would produce per kilowatt installed, per day. The data reveals that Venezuela’s solar resource is globally competitive, categorized by three main tiers:

  • Elite efficiency (2.9% of territory): Premium, high yield zones.
  • Immediate expansion (18.6% of territory): Centered in Zulia, Falcón and Guárico; ideal for powering states greatly affected by blackouts.
  • Widespread baseline (38.4% of territory): Proves viable solar infrastructure can be deployed across more than half of the country.

Wind Power

The map covers the capacity factor for a generic turbine, meaning the share of its maximum output delivered on average over the year. The data reveals that the strongest wind resource is concentrated in a few places, mostly on the Falcón coast, with a wider moderate resource inland:

  • Elite wind zones (1.6% of territory): World-class resource located primarily on the coasts of Falcón.
  • High-efficiency zones (1.7% of territory): Prime secondary locations for large-scale power grid injection.
  • Moderate wind zones (5.6% of territory): A wider, inland resource, mostly across Anzoátegui, Bolívar and Guárico; larger than the two top classes combined.

Notes for replication

The country outline, which cuts both layers and defines the land, is the boundary file supplied with the Global Solar and Wind Atlas download (World Bank Group). I used the state lines from Provita to add Guayana Esequiba to the base map. The region is shown in gray in both layers and is not counted in the shares. Although the wind data does not cover Guayana, but the solar data does, I decided to leave it out so that the two layers covered the same territory.

The solar data (photovoltaic output layer at 30 arc-seconds, about 1 km) comes from the Global Solar Atlas (World Bank Group, ESMAP and Solargis). The wind layer (turbine class IEC 3, at about 250 m) comes from the Global Wind Atlas. IEC 3 was chosen because it gives the highest capacity factor on 99.9% of the territory.

Both layers were cut to fit Venezuela’s map outline at about 909,000 km². Cells were averaged in blocks (about 3.7 km for solar and 3.3 km for wind) and kept if at least a quarter of it is land. Averages were grouped into six classes (in steps of 0.2 kWh per kWp per day for solar and 10 percentage points for wind). The class boundaries were drawn as slightly simplified polygons and specks smaller than six blocks were dropped. The lowest class of each layer is the color of the land itself.

The shares of the territory comes from the original, full-resolution layers.
