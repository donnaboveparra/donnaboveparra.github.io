Oro Negro:
Mapping the geography of Venezuela’s oil

Venezuela has long been a subject of media attention. However, starting in early 2026, the focus shifted: it is now primarily centered on our vast endowment of resources. Indeed, we possess the world’s largest proven oil reserves, exceeding 303 billion barrels. However, not everyone is familiar with the story of our oil–and the pride Venezuelans take in our heritage–nor where the oil is located, how it is refined, or whether our electrical grid is capable of supporting such operations.

I decided to conduct an exploratory analysis to gather all relevant information in one place. The project’s objective is to provide a resource that allows for the mapping of these assets, the monitoring of system operations, and to raise awareness regarding existing limitations, as well as how these might be addressed.

A brief history

The two most famous and consequential oil blowouts (reventones) in Venezuela’s history occurred in Zulia State, and they still shape the country’s economic and social course.

The data and design

The data used includes geospatial datasets and oil industry data obtained though Geoportal Provita, Global Energy Monitor (GEM), OPEC.

The design of the website is inspired by the Venezuelan flag and the kinetic and optical art of Carlos Cruz-Diez, both representing an extension of our identity.

How all the data was managed

I used Python (pandas, geopandas) through Google Colab to clean and merge datasets, as well as Altair, Vega-Lite and Visual Studio Code to create all visualizations. The workbooks and .json files can be accessed in the repository.

The resource

Oil production over time

The chart below highlights the most recent data on historical oil production from 1960 to 2025. Historical context allows to track the boom, bust and partial recovery of the country’s oil production. Ultimately, the data shows the dramatic decline in global standing, as the share of world crude output fell from 13.6% to a mere 1.6%.

Source: OPEC Annual Statistical Bulletin 2026, Table 3.5; share = Venezuela / world crude output. Event notes compiled from general historical sources.

Notes for replication

The data is extracted from Table 3.5 of OPEC’s Annual Statistical Bulletin 2026. No year was missing, and no value was changed. The share is calculated using Venezuela’s production divided by the world total for the same year, times 100, rounded to two decimals. Each year carries a historical note and are classified into four periods, coming from my own research and general background knowledge of Venezuelan history.

The geography of oil

The interactive map below tracks Venezuela’s oil blocks and fields by region. Field size can be viewed by oil in place (OPI) (the total estimated volume of crude physically trapped underground) and remaining recoverable (the fraction that can actually be brought to the surface using current technology). The data reveals that Zulia state holds the largest OIP volume at 266,563 million barrels (bbl) across 35 blocks and 25 fields, with 10,587 million bbl recoverable.

Blocks: Ministry of Energy and Petroleum via ECOSIG/Provita (records updated 2009 to 2018). Fields and reserves: Global Energy Monitor (GEM), Global Oil and Gas Extraction Tracker, March 2026 (reserve figures mostly from 2021). Some field locations added by the author.

Notes for replication

The state boundaries and oil blocks come from Provita. All names were translated from Spanish into English, and where relevant, all special characters were preserved. The following blocks were placed manually to correct the location based on the state border: Mene Grande and Catatumbo in Zulia, La Ceiba in Trujillo, and Moruy blocks I and IV in Falcón.

Some of GEM's oil and gas fields had no coordinates (latitude and longitude), so these were added based manually based on satellite data. Each field was matched to a block: first by lying inside it, then by the operator’s name, then by the nearest block.

Blocks and fields were grouped into regions to make the map easier to read for an international audience.

Our reserves

The chart illustrates the volume of proven oil reserves (in billions of bbl) for the top ten nations from 1960 to 2025. The data highlights Venezuela’s ascent from being a minor participant to the world leader, following the exploration of the Orinoco Oil Belt in 2010. Although OPEC’s reliance on self-reported data from member nations is frequently contested, a 2009 U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) fact sheet validates the claim, and estimates that the Orinoco Belt alone contains between 380 and 652 billion bbl of ultimately recoverable oil.

The system

The infrastructure network

The interactive map below tracks the refineries, upgraders and pipelines, as well as the blocks, by region. As shown in the map, Venezuela’s largest refinery is the Paraguaná Refinery Complex, having a nameplace capacity of 955,000 b/d and comprising the Amuay and Cardón refineries in Falcón state and the Bajo Grande refinery in Zulia state, being the largest in the Western Hemisphere and the second-largest in the world.

Sources: GEM Global Oil and Gas Infrastructure Tracker (oil and NGL pipelines, June 2026; gas pipelines, November 2025; LNG terminals, September 2025). Refineries and upgraders: Wikipedia (Paraguaná refinery complex), OverWatts, Reuters (2023; February 2026, El Palito and refineries in July 2026, Cardón in October 2026), La Patilla, Ursa Space; capacities are nameplate. Oil blocks: Ministry of Energy and Petroleum via ECOSIG/Provita (records updated 2009 to 2018).

Notes for replication

Each item was placed in a region based on its coordinates, following the same rules as visualization #2. The GEM trackers were filtered to all those running in Venezuela (i.e., excluding two gas pipelines running across borders, Trans-Caribbean and Dragon, and Venezuela-Colombia which was cancelled). All routes were simplified to about 200 meters.

The location of the refineries and upgraders were compiled from public sources, which are linked in the sources. All installed capacities are estimated, nameplate, not what they process. To improve the visualization, I only show Amuay and Cardón when zooming to Falcón and Paraguaná, given both are part of the Paraguaná Refinery Complex. The four upgraders at José share one location, so they are one dot on the map.

The refining gap

The chart illustrates the historical gap between Venezuela’s oil refining capacity and its actual throughput (the volume of crude oil processed) from 1980 through 2025, alongside a snapshot for 2026. The data reveals that this deficit has widened significantly over the decades, having come closest to closing only in 1991. This disparity is attributed by experts to underinvestment in the infrastructure network, being fueled by five key issues: decaying infrastructure, neglected maintenance, widespread equipment damage, frequent power disruptions, and critical material shortages.

Sources: Energy Institute (2026), Statistical Review of World Energy 2026, Energy Institute, London (refining capacity and refinery throughput, Venezuela, 1980 to 2025). 2026: Reuters, 8 April 2026, from workers at the refineries; Cardón fire: Reuters, 6 October 2026.

Notes for replication

The data comes from the Energy Institute’s Statistical Review of World Energy 2026. Capacity starts in 1965 and throughput in 1980, so the chart starts in 1980. Both series are complete for the 46 years from 1980 to 2025, and no value was filled in or changed. The 2026 snapshot comes from an article by Reuters; given the numbers are reported by workers, and not an annual average, it is included as a separate marker.

Capacity not in use (gap) was calculated as capacity minus throughput, and the share in use is throughput divided by capacity.

The proposal

Maracaibo calls itself “La Tierra del Sol Amada”. The proposal is to take advantage of that sun for the oil fields, with solar power and storage for oilfield loads.

Falcón is known for its wind. The refineries of Paraguaná sit on one of Venezuela’s windiest coasts, and wind power could serve the Paraguaná refining complex.
